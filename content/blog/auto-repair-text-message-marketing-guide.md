Most people don't check email until later in the day, if they check it at all. A text message usually gets read within minutes, somewhere around the same response time as "is my car ready yet." For a repair shop, that response speed is useful for more than just appointment confirmations, it's one of the simplest ways to keep bays full between the big marketing pushes.

Here's how to use it well, and how to avoid becoming the kind of business a customer blocks after one message.

## Why texting works so well for a repair shop

A car repair is rarely something someone's actively thinking about until it's urgent, or until a reminder nudges them. A text is short, immediate, and doesn't require someone to open an app or dig through a cluttered inbox. It's also a direct line that doesn't depend on an algorithm deciding whether to show your post, unlike social media, a text just arrives, no algorithm gets a vote.

## What to actually use it for

### Appointment reminders and confirmations

The simplest, most useful use of all. A quick "See you tomorrow at 10am for your brake service, reply to confirm" cuts down on no-shows and gives a customer an easy way to reschedule instead of just not showing up.

### Service completion notifications

"Your car's ready for pickup" beats a customer sitting in the waiting room checking the clock like it's a DMV line, or worse, not finding out until they call. It's a small thing that makes the whole experience feel more professional.

### Review requests

This is the direct link worth sending right at pickup, covered in more detail in our reviews guide. A text with a one-tap link to leave a Google review gets used far more often than the same ask by email.

### Win-back messages for past customers

A short, friendly nudge to someone who hasn't been in for a while, "It's been a year since your last oil change, want to grab a spot this week?", can bring back customers who simply forgot, not ones who went elsewhere on purpose.

### Occasional offers

Used sparingly, a seasonal reminder or limited-time discount works well by text. Used too often, it starts to feel like spam fast, and nobody's rebooking with the business that texts like a timeshare presentation, more on that below.

## Getting consent the right way

This part matters, both for trust and because it's legally required. Customers need to opt in before you text them for marketing purposes, a simple checkbox or verbal confirmation when they're in the shop works fine, "Can we text you appointment reminders and the occasional update?" Keep a record of who's agreed, and always make it easy to reply STOP to opt out. Skipping this step isn't worth the risk, and it's also just the respectful way to do it.

## How often to text

Far less than you'd post on social media or Google. Appointment-related texts are expected and welcome. Promotional texts should be rare, a few times a year at most, not a few times a month. The fastest way to get blocked is to turn a useful appointment reminder channel into a constant sales pitch, that's a one-way ticket to the "blocked" list, right next to the extended warranty robocalls.

## A few examples to borrow

**Appointment reminder:** "Hi [Name], this is [Shop] confirming your brake service tomorrow at 10am. Reply YES to confirm or call us to reschedule."

**Ready for pickup:** "Your car is all set, [Name]! Ready for pickup whenever works for you today."

**Review request:** "Thanks for trusting us with your car, [Name]! If you have 30 seconds, we'd really appreciate a quick review: [link]"

**Win-back:** "Hi [Name], it's been a while since your last visit. If your car's due for a check-up, we'd love to see you again: [link or number]"

## Where to start this week

1. **Add a simple opt-in question** at checkout or on your intake form.
2. **Start with appointment reminders**, the easiest, most obviously useful starting point.
3. **Add review request texts** right after pickup.
4. **Save a short list of win-back customers** to text once you're comfortable with the basics.
