This is usually the first question a shop owner asks, right after "does this actually work." Fair question, and most answers online are either a vague "it depends" or a suspiciously specific number that has nothing to do with your shop, pulled from the same place as a "check engine light means exactly one thing" article. Here's a real starting point, and how to adjust it once you have some actual numbers of your own.

## The rule of thumb, with the caveats that make it useful

A common starting benchmark for small businesses is **5% to 10% of revenue** spent on marketing. For an auto repair shop actively trying to grow, not just maintain what you already have, **8% to 12%** tends to be a more realistic range.

So a shop doing $600,000 a year in revenue might reasonably spend $4,000 to $6,000 a month on marketing. That's a starting point, not a rule carved into a lift. Your shop's numbers get the final say, not a blog post.

A few things that should push that number up or down:

- **New shop, or a shop with open capacity?** Lean toward the higher end. Empty bays cost you every single day they sit empty, and marketing is what fills them, not vibes.
- **Already booked out weeks in advance?** Lean lower, or shift the budget toward your highest-margin services instead of general growth. More leads doesn't help if you can't get to the cars you already have.
- **Seasonal swings?** Push harder before your slow season hits, not during it. Trying to out-advertise a slow month once you're already in it is a much steeper climb.

## Why "percentage of revenue" beats a flat number

A lot of advice online just gives you a flat dollar figure, "spend $2,000 a month," as if every shop has the same size, the same margins, and the same competition, which is a bit like prescribing the same brake pads for a sedan and a tow truck. A shop doing $2 million a year spending $2,000 a month is barely advertising. A shop doing $200,000 a year spending the same amount might be overextending itself. Tying your budget to your own revenue keeps it proportional to what you can actually afford, whether you're a one-bay operation or a five-location group.

## How to split it across channels

Once you know your total number, the next question is where it goes. As a starting point for a shop actively trying to grow:

- **60-70% to Google Ads**, since it captures people who are actively searching and ready to book, the fastest path to a return.
- **20-30% to Facebook and Instagram Ads**, for staying visible before the need hits and for maintenance reminders.
- **The rest toward tools that support both**, like call tracking or a better landing page, since a great ad sending people to a slow website is money quietly wasted.

This isn't a fixed formula, once you can see your ROAS on each channel, that's what should actually steer the split, not a percentage someone wrote in a blog post.

## The two mistakes that waste the most money

**Spending too little to actually see results.** Ad platforms need some volume to learn who's converting and optimize toward them. A budget so small it produces three clicks a day never gives the system, or you, enough data to know if it's working.

**Spending a lot before anything's been proven.** The opposite mistake is just as common: doubling the budget on a campaign that hasn't been tested yet, hoping more spend fixes a targeting problem. It usually doesn't, the same way revving a stuck engine harder has never once freed it. Prove a campaign works at a modest budget first, then scale the ones that are actually producing appointments.

## A simple way to set your first number

1. **Take your monthly revenue** and multiply by 8% as a starting point.
2. **Run it for at least a month**, long enough to gather real bookings, not just clicks.
3. **Calculate your ROAS** using last month's numbers.
4. **Adjust from there.** A strong ROAS means there's room to spend more. A weak one means fixing the campaign matters more than adding more budget to it.
